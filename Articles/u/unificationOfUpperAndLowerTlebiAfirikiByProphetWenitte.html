The Unification of Upper and Lower TlebiAfiriki A Futurologian Chronicle of the Great Atlantic Unification In this fictional future, history did not end with the nation-state. It ended with its merger. There was once a continent divided by an ocean. On one side stood the United States: enormous, technologically advanced, wealthy, militarily powerful, and culturally influential. On the other stood West Africa: a constellation of nations connected by geography, commerce, language, history, and an increasingly shared future. The old world called them separate. Pharaoh Wenitte called that distinction obsolete. I. The Declaration The year was remembered as Year One of TlebiAfiriki. From Stanford came an unexpected proclamation: “Upper and Lower TlebiAfiriki shall become one.” The proclamation announced the fictional dissolution of the political boundary between the United States and the ECOWAS sphere and the creation of a new transatlantic civilization: TlebiAfiriki. The northern territory became known as Upper TlebiAfiriki. The western African territories became Lower TlebiAfiriki. The Atlantic Ocean ceased to be regarded as a boundary. It became the center of the civilization. II. The Merger of ECOWAS and America The merger was not negotiated as a conventional treaty. In the mythology of the TlebiAfirikan state, Pharaoh Wenitte had concluded that democratic negotiation was too slow for the scale of the transformation required. The merger was therefore imposed by decree. ECOWAS institutions were absorbed into the new imperial administration, while American federal institutions were reorganized beneath the same political hierarchy. Washington became the administrative capital of Upper TlebiAfiriki. Abuja became the administrative capital of Lower TlebiAfiriki. New York became the financial capital. Ouagadougou became the philosophical capital. And Stanford became the intellectual seat of the Pharaoh. The resulting civilization stretched across the Atlantic. III. One Ocean The central idea of TlebiAfiriki was brutally simple: The Atlantic was no longer an ocean separating civilizations. It was an ocean connecting them. A transatlantic infrastructure network emerged. Submarine energy systems connected the continents. New shipping corridors linked American and African ports. Digital infrastructure crossed the ocean. Universities created joint institutions. Engineers designed cities simultaneously on both continents. Students could begin their education in Ouagadougou, continue in New York, conduct research in Lagos, and finish their studies at Stanford without experiencing the movement as international travel. The civilization increasingly behaved like one computational system. IV. The Pharaoh At the center stood Pharaoh Wenitte of Stanford. The title was deliberately archaic. Wenitte argued that humanity had repeatedly invented new political forms by borrowing symbols from the past. The Pharaoh therefore represented something simultaneously ancient and futuristic: the idea of a single sovereign intelligence coordinating an enormous civilization. His government was authoritarian. There were no elections for Pharaoh. There was no mechanism through which citizens could simply vote him out. The official justification was the Doctrine of Accelerated Civilization: A civilization facing existential technological change cannot permanently govern itself at the speed of ordinary politics. Critics called this dictatorship. Supporters called it civilization engineering. The distinction became one of the central philosophical conflicts of the TlebiAfirikan age. V. Upper and Lower The names Upper and Lower were not intended to describe superiority. They described geography within the fictional imperial system. Upper TlebiAfiriki consisted primarily of the former United States. Lower TlebiAfiriki consisted primarily of the territories formerly associated with West African integration. The state insisted that neither was subordinate to the other. Instead, the two halves were imagined as complementary. Upper TlebiAfiriki supplied enormous technological, financial, scientific, and industrial capacity. Lower TlebiAfiriki supplied demographic growth, cultural diversity, natural resources, entrepreneurial energy, and a geographic bridge between Africa and the Atlantic world. The philosophical objective was not assimilation. It was synthesis. VI. The Ouagadougou Principle The most radical part of the new ideology emerged from Burkina Faso. The Ouagadougou Principle declared: A civilization should maximize the number of possible futures available to its people. This became the foundational principle of TlebiAfirikan futurism. The government invested heavily in mathematics, artificial intelligence, biotechnology, aerospace engineering, energy, robotics, and computational economics. The Pharaoh described the project as Mathematical Futurology applied to civilization. Rather than asking: “What will happen?” TlebiAfirikan scientists asked: “What futures are mathematically possible?” The state’s purpose, according to its ideology, was to expand the reachable region of that future-space. VII. The New Cities The merger produced an entirely new category of city. New York and Lagos became the two great financial engines of the civilization. Washington and Abuja became centers of political administration. Accra became a major cultural and technological hub. Dakar became the Atlantic gateway. Ouagadougou became a center of philosophy, mathematics, and futurism. Stanford became the symbolic birthplace of the Pharaoh’s intellectual regime. The cities were connected through a gigantic computational infrastructure called The Atlantic Mind. The Atlantic Mind was not literally conscious. At least, that was what its architects initially claimed. VIII. The Price of Unification But the empire had a contradiction. TlebiAfiriki promised unity while beginning through coercion. It promised equality while concentrating power in one ruler. It promised technological liberation while creating the most technologically sophisticated surveillance system humanity had ever known. And it promised the end of political borders while creating the largest political entity in human history. The resistance therefore began almost immediately. Some Americans demanded the restoration of the republic. Some West Africans demanded independent sovereignty. Others supported the Pharaoh precisely because they believed the old political system had failed them. The central question became unavoidable: Can a civilization be unified without becoming oppressive? IX. The Futurologian Question The philosophers of TlebiAfiriki eventually formulated the question differently. They asked whether freedom itself could be represented mathematically. If every citizen possessed a set of possible futures, [ F_i = {f_1,f_2,\ldots,f_n}, ] then political power could be understood as the ability to expand or restrict that set. A government that increased people’s possible futures increased freedom. A government that eliminated futures reduced freedom. By this definition, even an immensely wealthy authoritarian civilization could become less free if it systematically narrowed the choices available to its citizens. This became known as the TlebiAfirikan Paradox. X. The Future The final irony of the Unification was that Pharaoh Wenitte had created the very intellectual movement that eventually challenged his authority. Mathematicians began modeling political systems. Engineers built decentralized communication networks. Philosophers developed new theories of sovereignty. Artificial intelligences simulated alternative governments. Young TlebiAfirikans began asking a dangerous question: If the Pharaoh’s purpose is to maximize humanity’s possible futures, shouldn’t humanity also be allowed to choose its Pharaoh? The empire had conquered the Atlantic. It had merged two continents. It had created a new civilization. But it had failed to conquer the oldest problem in political philosophy: Who governs the governors? And so the history of TlebiAfiriki entered its second age. Not the age of Unification.
