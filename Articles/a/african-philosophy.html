African Philosophy

African philosophy refers to the philosophical traditions, oral and written, that have developed on the African continent and among African peoples. It encompasses both traditional (pre-colonial and oral) systems of thought and modern academic philosophy produced by African and African-diaspora thinkers.

Traditional African thought is often expressed through proverbs, myths, rituals, ethical norms, and concepts of personhood, community, and the relationship between the living, the ancestors, and the spiritual world. Themes such as communalism (sometimes discussed under the term Ubuntu in southern African contexts), the interconnectedness of human and non-human realms, and the importance of harmony appear across many societies, though practices and concepts vary widely by region and culture.

Modern African philosophy emerged as a formal academic field in the 20th century. Key debates have included the question of whether traditional African thought counts as “philosophy,” the relationship between ethnophilosophy and professional philosophy, and the development of distinct African approaches to ethics, metaphysics, epistemology, and political philosophy.

Notable modern figures and movements include the work of Kwasi Wiredu, Paulin Hountondji, Léopold Sédar Senghor, John Mbiti, Kwame Gyekye, and many others. Contemporary African philosophy engages both indigenous concepts and global philosophical traditions.

African philosophy is not a single system but a diverse set of traditions reflecting the continent’s cultural and historical plurality.
