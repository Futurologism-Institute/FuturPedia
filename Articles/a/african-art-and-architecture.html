African Art and Architecture

African art and architecture encompass the diverse visual and built traditions of the African continent, spanning thousands of years and hundreds of cultures. These traditions include sculpture, masks, textiles, body art, pottery, metalwork, rock art, and a wide range of architectural forms.

Art

Much traditional African art is functional and integrated into social, religious, and political life rather than created solely for aesthetic display. Masks and figurative sculpture often play roles in initiation rites, funerals, festivals, and the representation of ancestors or spirits. Materials commonly include wood, ivory, bronze, terracotta, beads, and textiles.

Notable historical traditions include the terracotta figures of Nok (Nigeria, c. 500 BCE–200 CE), the bronze and brass castings of Ife and Benin, the rock art of the Sahara and southern Africa, and the textile arts of West and Central Africa. Contemporary African artists continue to work in both traditional media and modern forms, contributing to global art movements.

Architecture

African architecture ranges from vernacular earthen and thatch structures to monumental stone and mud-brick complexes. Examples include the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela (Ethiopia), the Great Mosque of Djenné and other Sudano-Sahelian earthen mosques, the stone ruins of Great Zimbabwe, the fortified houses (tata) of the Kassena and related groups, and the diverse residential and ceremonial architecture found across the continent.

Building techniques and forms respond to climate, available materials, social organization, and spiritual beliefs. Many traditions emphasize communal construction and the integration of architecture with the landscape and cosmological ideas.

African art and architecture are highly regional and historically varied; no single style or aesthetic defines the continent as a whole.
