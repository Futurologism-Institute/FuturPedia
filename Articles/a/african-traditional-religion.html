African Traditional Religion

African Traditional Religion (ATR) is a collective term used by scholars for the indigenous religious beliefs and practices of African peoples that predate and exist alongside Islam and Christianity. It is not a single, unified religion but a diverse family of related traditions found across the continent.

Common features identified in many (though not all) African traditional systems include:

  • Belief in a Supreme Being or Creator, often distant from everyday affairs
  • Veneration of ancestors as intermediaries and guardians of the community
  • Recognition of lesser spirits or divinities associated with natural forces, places, or human activities
  • Importance of ritual, sacrifice, and divination for maintaining harmony between the human, natural, and spiritual worlds
  • Emphasis on community, moral order, and the consequences of actions for both the living and the dead

Practices vary significantly by ethnic group, region, and historical period. Oral transmission, rather than written scripture, has been the primary means of preserving and teaching these traditions.

In the modern era, African Traditional Religions continue to be practiced, often in interaction or syncretism with Christianity and Islam. Academic study of ATR developed in the 20th century through the work of scholars such as John Mbiti, Bolaji Idowu, and others.

The term “African Traditional Religion” is a scholarly construct; individual communities typically refer to their own specific practices and names for spiritual realities.
