Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980)

Jean-Paul Sartre was a French philosopher, novelist, playwright, and public intellectual, and one of the central figures of 20th-century existentialism.

In Being and Nothingness (1943) Sartre developed a phenomenological ontology distinguishing being-in-itself (the non-conscious) from being-for-itself (consciousness). He argued that human beings are radically free and that “existence precedes essence”: there is no predetermined human nature; we create ourselves through our choices.

Sartre emphasized responsibility, authenticity, and the phenomenon of “bad faith” (self-deception about one’s freedom). His political writings and plays (including No Exit and The Flies) explored freedom, commitment, and the gaze of the Other.

He declined the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1964. Sartre remained a prominent left-wing intellectual and public figure throughout his life.

Key works: Being and Nothingness, Existentialism Is a Humanism, Critique of Dialectical Reason, and numerous novels and plays.
