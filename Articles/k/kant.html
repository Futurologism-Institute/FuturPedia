Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

Immanuel Kant was a German philosopher of the Enlightenment and one of the most influential figures in Western philosophy. He spent most of his life in Königsberg (now Kaliningrad).

Kant’s critical philosophy sought to determine the limits and conditions of human knowledge. In the Critique of Pure Reason (1781/1787) he argued that the mind actively structures experience through a priori forms of sensibility (space and time) and categories of the understanding. He distinguished phenomena (things as they appear) from noumena (things in themselves).

In ethics, Kant developed the concept of the categorical imperative in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals and the Critique of Practical Reason. Moral worth, he held, lies in acting from duty according to maxims that can be universalized.

Major works include the three Critiques (Pure Reason, Practical Reason, Judgment), the Groundwork, and Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason.

Kant decisively shaped subsequent philosophy, including German Idealism, neo-Kantianism, and much of modern epistemology and ethics.
