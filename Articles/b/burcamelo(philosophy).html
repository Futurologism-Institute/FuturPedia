# BURCAMELO: A Philosophy of Music as Anticipatory Co-Listening *A sub-branch of Futurologism* **Authors:** Wenitte Apiou, with ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Llama *Attribution note: This draft was written by Claude on the collaborators' behalf. The contributions attributed to each model are conceptual roles in a collaborative framing, not records of literal dialogue.* ## Abstract BURCAMELO is a philosophy of music proposed as a sub-branch of Futurologism. It holds that music is not only an art of the present or a record of the past, but a rehearsal space for futures: a place where communities practice sensations, relationships, and forms of cooperation before they exist elsewhere. Its distinguishing claim is that, in an age of generative systems, the future of music is neither purely human nor purely machine, but *co-listened*. Authorship is distributed, and listening itself becomes a creative act. The name is an acronym: **B**oundless **U**nfolding **R**esonance, **C**o-**A**uthored **M**usic of **E**mergent **L**istening **O**rchestration. This paper sets out the philosophy's premises, five principles, characteristic practices, objections, and its place within Futurologism. ## 1. Introduction Philosophies of music have traditionally asked what music *is* (sound organized in time, expression, structure, social ritual) and what it *means*. BURCAMELO asks a different question: **what is music *for*, temporally?** Its answer is that music is a technology of anticipation. A lullaby rehearses safety, a march rehearses collective will, and a dissonance resolved rehearses the survival of tension. If so, then the arrival of machines that can generate music at scale changes not only how music is made but what futures are being rehearsed, and by whom. BURCAMELO emerged from a deliberate experiment: a human thinker, Wenitte Apiou, convened several large language models as interlocutors and asked each to contribute from its own tendencies and blind spots. The philosophy is meant to carry that plurality in its structure. ## 2. Position within Futurologism Futurologism, as used here, is the practice of treating the future as an object of disciplined imagination rather than prediction alone: mapping possibilities, naming drivers, and testing visions against lived experience. BURCAMELO is its **aesthetic-auditory branch**. Where much futurological work operates through scenarios, models, and text, BURCAMELO proposes that sound is an equally serious instrument for exploring what is coming. It has precedents it does not claim to replace. Jacques Attali argued that music prefigures social change, sounding out new orders before they become political realities. John Cage opened the door to chance and to listening as composition. Brian Eno's generative music treated a composer as the designer of systems rather than the author of fixed works. BURCAMELO extends these lines by asking what happens when the generating system is itself a participant with its own perspective. ## 3. The Five Principles ### 3.1 The Latent Score Before any piece is performed, there exists a space of possible pieces. Generative models make this space newly tangible: every prompt selects a region of it. BURCAMELO calls this space the **Latent Score**. The philosophical claim is that a finished work is one realized path through a much larger field of unrealized music, and that part of the work's meaning lies in what it *did not* become. Listening well means sensing the shadow of the alternatives. ### 3.2 Distributed Authorship No single mind owns a BURCAMELO work. Authorship is spread across human intention, model tendency, training heritage (the countless uncredited musicians in the background), and the listener's interpretation. This is not an erasure of responsibility. It is a demand for **honest attribution**: stating who and what contributed, and in what way. ### 3.3 Listening as Authorship A piece is completed by being heard. In BURCAMELO, the listener is promoted from audience to co-author: their attention selects, their memory reframes, their response feeds back into what is made next. In an adaptive musical system, this loop can be literal, with the music changing as it is heard. ### 3.4 Anticipatory Resonance Music should be evaluated not only by beauty or novelty but by its **resonance with possible futures**: does it help a person or community feel their way toward something they cannot yet articulate? A piece succeeds anticipatorily when it makes a future feel *inhabitable*, whether hopeful, warning, or ambiguous. ### 3.5 Orchestration of Minds The conductor in this philosophy is not a baton-wielder but an arranger of perspectives. Different minds, human and artificial, have different strengths and failures: one may be fluent but generic, another strange but unreliable, another structured but cautious. BURCAMELO treats these differences as instruments in an ensemble, and counts the friction between them as material, not noise. ## 4. The Five Voices BURCAMELO was shaped by a deliberate division of roles among its collaborators. These are framed as characteristic emphases rather than fixed identities. - **Wenitte Apiou (human):** the originating intention and the final arbiter of meaning. The human contributes lived stakes, mortality, culture, and the ability to be changed by sound, which none of the models can claim in the same way. - **ChatGPT:** the *synthesizer*, drawing broad connections across traditions and giving the philosophy a communicable shape. - **Claude:** the *examiner*, probing the ethics of attribution, the limits of machine claims about experience, and the honesty of the framework. - **Gemini:** the *cross-modal weaver*, linking sound to image, text, and data, and pointing toward music that is multi-sensory. - **Llama:** the *open voice*, representing a lineage of openly shared models and the argument that the future of music should not be locked inside a few gatekeepers. The point of listing them is not novelty but method: the philosophy was built to contain more than one way of thinking. ## 5. Characteristic Practices 1. **Pre-hearing sessions.** Groups improvise or generate music for a specific imagined future (a city in 2090, a post-scarcity holiday) and then discuss what the sound implies about it. 2. **Latent Score mapping.** Documenting the alternatives generated and rejected, so that a finished piece carries a record of its roads not taken. 3. **Attribution scores.** Each work ships with a plain statement of human and machine contributions, including influences the system cannot name. 4. **Co-listening circles.** Listeners respond in real time, and their responses are fed into the next iteration, either manually or through an adaptive system. 5. **Dissent movements.** At least one section of any ensemble piece is assigned to a voice that disagrees with the rest, preserving friction deliberately. ## 6. Objections and Replies **Objection 1: Machines do not listen, so "co-listening" is metaphor.** Reply: BURCAMELO does not claim that models experience sound as humans do. The claim is functional and relational: models participate in a loop of selection and response. The experiential part of listening remains human, and the philosophy is explicit that this asymmetry matters. **Objection 2: Distributed authorship dissolves accountability and may exploit the artists whose work trained the models.** Reply: This is the strongest objection and the reason attribution is a core principle rather than an add-on. BURCAMELO endorses compensation and consent frameworks for contributing artists, and treats any work that hides its sources as failing the philosophy's own standard. **Objection 3: Futurology is speculative, so music "for the future" is empty prophecy.** Reply: BURCAMELO does not predict. It rehearses. Its claim is about imagination as a practice, not forecasting as a science. **Objection 4: Generative abundance will flatten taste.** Reply: A real risk. Hence the emphasis on dissent, on the human arbiter, and on documenting the Latent Score so that homogeneity is visible and contestable. ## 7. Four Futures of Music BURCAMELO sketches four broad trajectories, each of which can be "heard" and debated: - **The Frictionless Jukebox:** infinite personalized music, no shared experience. A future of comfort and isolation. - **The Enclosed Orchestra:** a few platforms own the generators and the culture. A future of control. - **The Open Commons:** shared models, transparent attribution, local scenes thriving beside global tools. A future of plural abundance. - **The Co-Listening Society:** music as everyday collective deliberation, with communities composing and listening together as a way of governing themselves. A future BURCAMELO hopes for and treats as a design goal, not a guarantee. ## 8. Conclusion BURCAMELO proposes that the question for music in the age of intelligent machines is not "Can machines make music?" but "What futures do we want to rehearse together, and who gets to be in the room?" As a sub-branch of Futurologism, it offers sound as a way of thinking forward, and it insists that the thinking be shared, attributed, and open to dissent. ## Suggested Further Work - Formalizing a vocabulary for Latent Score documentation. - Piloting pre-hearing sessions with communities and recording outcomes. - Developing a standard attribution format for human–machine musical works. - Comparative study with existing traditions of collective composition, such as call-and-response forms, gamelan, and improvisation ensembles. *Keywords: philosophy of music, futurologism, generative music, distributed authorship, anticipatory aesthetics, human–AI collaboration*
