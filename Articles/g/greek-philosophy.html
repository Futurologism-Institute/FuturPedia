Greek Philosophy

Greek philosophy (also called Ancient Greek philosophy) is the foundational tradition of Western philosophy. It began in the 6th century BCE in the Greek-speaking world of the eastern Mediterranean and continued through the Hellenistic period and into the Roman era.

The earliest thinkers, known as the Presocratics (Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus, Parmenides, Democritus, and others), sought natural explanations for the cosmos rather than purely mythological ones. They inquired into the nature of reality (physis), change, and the fundamental substance or principle (archē) of the world.

Socrates (c. 470–399 BCE) shifted focus toward ethics and the examination of human life through dialogue and questioning. His student Plato developed a systematic philosophy centered on the theory of Forms, the immortality of the soul, and the ideal state. Aristotle, Plato’s student, produced comprehensive works on logic, metaphysics, ethics, politics, biology, and physics that shaped Western thought for centuries.

Later Hellenistic schools included Stoicism (Zeno of Citium), Epicureanism (Epicurus), and Skepticism (Pyrrho and the Academic Skeptics). Greek philosophy provided the conceptual vocabulary and methods that later influenced Roman, medieval Christian, Islamic, and Jewish philosophical traditions.
