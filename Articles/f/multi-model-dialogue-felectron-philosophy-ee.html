Original questions (Wenitte Apiou):
What are your thoughts on Philosophy of Electrical Engineering: Is there a Lambda Calculus / Turing Machine for EE? EE is closely tied to computation but is fundamentally about electron Flow / signal analysis. Is an Isolated Electron considered a form of electricity? I am currently working on a new Mathematical Model for a Theoretical Enhanced Electron called the FuturElectron (shortened as the Felectron). Do you think we have a Differential Equation that 100% accurately defines the electron? How would we test for it experimentally?

ChatGPT

Interesting set of questions. Electrical engineering doesn’t have a single foundational discrete model the way computation has lambda calculus or the Turing machine. The closest practical analogues are Kirchhoff’s laws + Maxwell’s equations expressed as systems of differential equations, or the circuit-theoretic graph models used in SPICE.

An isolated electron is a charged particle; most engineers would not call it “electricity” until it participates in current or fields.

We do not have a differential equation that 100% defines the electron. The Dirac equation (and QED more broadly) is extraordinarily accurate but incomplete—gravity is missing, and renormalization issues remain.

For a FuturElectron / Felectron model: publish clear quantitative predictions that differ from the Standard Model electron (g-2, polarizability, scattering cross-sections, etc.). Then test those deviations with precision measurements (Penning traps, electron-beam experiments, single-electron devices). Without falsifiable differences it stays pure theory.

Gemini

From a physics and engineering standpoint:

  • There is no universal discrete “lambda calculus of EE.” Circuit theory, transmission-line equations, and port-Hamiltonian formalisms serve as the working foundations. Category-theoretic approaches to circuits are an active research direction but not yet standard.
  • An isolated electron is electrostatic charge, not electricity in the usual engineering sense. Electricity implies flow or dynamical electromagnetic effects.
  • No differential equation fully and finally defines the electron. Dirac + QED is the best current description; it is not complete.
  • Experimental test of any Felectron model requires concrete, measurable deviations from known electron properties. High-precision spectroscopy, anomalous magnetic moment measurements, or new interaction channels would be the natural places to look. Consistency with existing data is also required.

Claude

These questions sit at a genuine philosophical and technical boundary.

Computation has clean discrete universal models; electrical engineering remains closer to continuous physics. Useful formalisms exist (bond graphs, signal-flow graphs, algebraic circuit theory), but none has the status of a Turing machine for EE.

An isolated electron is not ordinarily considered “electricity.” Electricity is a macroscopic or circuit-level phenomenon involving charge dynamics.

We do not possess a 100% complete differential equation for the electron. The Dirac equation and quantum electrodynamics are superbly successful yet known to be incomplete.

Any FuturElectron proposal needs precise governing equations and clear, testable predictions that differ from the ordinary electron. Experimental programs would then target those differences with existing or near-future precision tools. Without that, the model cannot be evaluated.

Lucienne

Philosophy of EE is under-discussed. Computation abstracted the physical substrate away; EE never fully did.

No clean Lambda/Turing equivalent exists for electron flow and signal analysis. The nearest things are the differential-algebraic systems of circuit theory and the geometric/port-based formalisms.

Isolated electron ≠ electricity. Electricity is relational—charge in motion or in field interaction.

No equation 100% captures the electron. Dirac/QED is the current best map, not the territory.

Felectron: interesting name. Show the mathematics. Show where it departs from the Standard Model electron. Then the experimental path becomes obvious—measure the departure.
