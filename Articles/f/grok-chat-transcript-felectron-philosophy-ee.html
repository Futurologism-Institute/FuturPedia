Thoughts on the post

The post raises several interconnected questions about the philosophy/foundations of electrical engineering (EE), the status of the electron, and a proposed theoretical entity called the “FuturElectron” / “Felectron.” Here’s a direct take.

Is there a Lambda Calculus / Turing Machine equivalent for EE?

Not in a clean, widely accepted form the way lambda calculus and Turing machines serve as foundational models of computation.

  • Computation has discrete, symbolic models (lambda calculus, Turing machines, combinatory logic, etc.) that abstract away the physical substrate.
  • EE is fundamentally about continuous physical phenomena: electromagnetic fields, charge flow, energy, signals, and circuit behavior governed by Maxwell’s equations, Kirchhoff’s laws, transmission-line theory, etc.
  • There are formalisms that play analogous roles inside EE and related fields:
    • Circuit theory itself (graph-theoretic + differential-equation models).
    • Signal-flow graphs, bond graphs, and port-Hamiltonian systems for energy and signal flow.
    • SPICE-style netlist + numerical solvers as a practical “machine” model.
    • Quantum and semi-classical models when you go down to electron transport, tunneling, etc.
    • Category-theoretic or algebraic approaches to circuits (e.g., work by Baez, Fong, and others on compositional circuit theory).

These are useful, but none has the same status as a universal discrete model of computation. EE remains closer to continuous physics than to pure discrete mathematics.

Is an isolated electron a form of electricity?

By conventional definition, no.

  • Electricity usually refers to the flow or effects of charge (current, voltage, electromagnetic fields, power transfer). Static charge is electrostatics; moving charge is current.
  • A single free electron at rest is a charged particle. It produces an electric field, but most engineers would not call that “electricity” in the sense of the phenomena EE studies.
  • Once it moves, interacts with other charges, or is part of a current or circuit, it becomes relevant to electrical phenomena. In vacuum tubes, particle accelerators, or single-electron transistors we do treat individual electrons as the carriers of electrical effects, but the isolated particle itself is still just a charge.

Do we have a differential equation that 100% accurately defines the electron?

No.

  • The Dirac equation (and its non-relativistic limit, the Schrödinger equation) describes the quantum behavior of an electron extremely well under a wide range of conditions.
  • Quantum electrodynamics (QED) gives a more complete relativistic quantum-field description that includes interactions with the electromagnetic field, virtual particles, anomalous magnetic moment, etc.
  • These are the best theories we have, and they are extraordinarily accurate, but they are not “100% complete.” They do not incorporate gravity, they leave open questions about the electron’s self-energy, and they sit inside a larger Standard Model that still has free parameters and known limitations (neutrino masses, hierarchy problems, dark matter/energy, etc.).
  • There is no single classical differential equation that fully captures the electron; classical models (Lorentz force, etc.) are approximations.

Experimental tests for a “FuturElectron / Felectron”

Any new mathematical model of an “enhanced” electron needs clear, falsifiable predictions that differ from the Standard Model electron (or from QED). Useful tests would look for:

  • Deviations in the electron’s magnetic moment (g-2), charge radius, or polarizability beyond current QED precision.
  • Anomalous scattering cross-sections or energy-loss behavior in high-precision experiments.
  • New effects in single-electron transistors, quantum dots, or precision spectroscopy.
  • Predicted new particles, resonances, or interaction channels that current accelerators or table-top experiments could constrain.
  • Consistency checks against existing high-precision data (atomic spectra, Penning-trap measurements, electron-beam experiments, etc.).

Without concrete, quantitative differences from the ordinary electron that can be measured (or bounded) experimentally, the model remains pure theory.

Overall impression

The questions are interesting and sit at the boundary of physics, EE, and foundations. The idea of a more complete “computational” or algebraic foundation for EE is a legitimate research direction (compositional systems theory, applied category theory, etc.). The “FuturElectron” idea needs a precise mathematical definition and clear experimental signatures before it can be evaluated seriously.

If you (or the author) share more details of the Felectron model—governing equations, predicted deviations, etc.—I’d be happy to look at them more carefully.
