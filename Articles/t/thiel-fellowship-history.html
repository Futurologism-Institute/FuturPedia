Thiel Fellowship — History

Founded: Announced September 2010; first class 2011
Founder: Peter Thiel (via the Thiel Foundation)
Original name: 20 Under 20
Early operators: Michael Gibson, Danielle Strachman
Historical overview for FuturPedia. Companion to Thiel Fellowship and Alumni. Drawn from public accounts (program announcements, Wikipedia, contemporaneous reporting, and later retrospectives).

Origin (September 2010)

The idea took shape on a flight to San Francisco the day before TechCrunch Disrupt. Peter Thiel was traveling with colleagues including Jim O’Neill, Jonathan Cain, and Luke Nosek. Discussion turned on technological stagnation outside computing and on higher education as a default path rather than a necessary one.

Thiel had considered founding a university and rejected it as misaligned with his view of education. Reading the script of The Social Network—which portrayed him unfavorably—fed interest in a public, contrarian project. The group framed the concept as an “anti-Rhodes Scholarship”: support talented young people who would leave school to build.

The program was announced on 29 September 2010 as 20 Under 20: up to twenty people under age twenty, a grant of $100,000 over two years, mentorship from Thiel’s network, and no equity taken by the fellowship itself.

Michael Gibson (hired the day before to help Thiel teach at Stanford and work with Thiel Capital) was pulled in immediately to design the program. About a week later, Danielle Strachman was hired to lead design and operations. Both later co-founded 1517 Fund.

Early years (2011–2014)

MilestoneDetail

Third classAnnounced May 2013 (22 fellows; seven outside the United States)
Fourth classAnnounced June 2014

Thesis: Outstanding founders and builders do not require university degrees; breakthrough work often comes from people institutions undervalue. Eligible projects included startups, scientific research, and social ventures. The cultural signal was to build rather than default to the classroom.

Early friction: Contemporary and later accounts describe thin structure at the start, gaps in housing and community, uneven support, and mixed cohort outcomes. Public criticism was sharp; in 2013 Larry Summers called the program among the most misdirected philanthropy of the decade. Defenders treated it as a long-horizon experiment rather than a two-year accelerator with immediate demo-day metrics.

2015 turning point

  • Age limit raised to 22.
  • Application and cohort processes adjusted over time (including greater flexibility and, eventually, rolling applications).
  • Strachman and Gibson left day-to-day Fellowship leadership and, with Thiel’s backing, founded 1517 Fund to extend the same talent thesis through venture capital.
  • Program leadership under the Thiel Foundation was refreshed.

Scale and outcomes

By the mid-2020s the Fellowship had supported 300+ fellows (counts vary slightly by cutoff date). Results followed a power-law pattern: a small set of alumni drove outsized attention and economic value.

Selected notable alumni by approximate fellowship year:

  • 2011: Laura Deming (Longevity Fund); Dale Stephens (UnCollege / Year On)
  • 2012: Dylan Field (Figma); Chris Olah (later Anthropic); Taylor Wilson
  • 2013: Austin Russell (Luminar); Ritesh Agarwal (OYO)
  • 2014: Vitalik Buterin (Ethereum); Lucy Guo (Scale AI)
  • Later: Boyan Slat (The Ocean Cleanup); Joshua Browder (DoNotPay); and many others

Secondary reporting (e.g. Washington Post, 2023) cited on the order of eleven unicorn founders among a few hundred recipients at that time; program-adjacent materials have claimed higher unicorn counts as portfolios matured. Aggregate company-value figures in the press are directional and method-dependent.

Danielle Strachman’s framing—a two-year program with a ten-year timeline—captures the intended evaluation horizon. Longer-term commentary has generally held that the Fellowship does not prove college is bad for everyone, but that an alternate track can work extremely well for a small, selected set.

Recent structure (2025–2026)

  • Grant increased to $250,000 over two years (from $100,000), especially visible for the 2026 class.
  • Eligibility (program FAQ): age 22 or younger, no university degree; must leave school if selected; fellowship does not take equity.
  • Cohorts remain small and highly selective.
  • Recent classes show more global participation and a stronger hard-tech / deep-tech emphasis (robotics, physical AI, biotech, infrastructure) alongside software.

Institutional family

  • Thiel Foundation — sponsors the Fellowship
  • Thiel Fellowship — grants, community, brand
  • 1517 Fund (2015–) — Strachman and Gibson; continuity of dropout / deep-tech thesis via venture capital

Reception arc

  1. 2010–2012: Shock, media debate, higher-education backlash
  2. 2013–2015: “Where are the results?” and internal growing pains
  3. 2016–2023: Alumni outcomes reframe the narrative
  4. 2024–2026: Normalized as a top-tier talent pipeline; larger checks; harder-tech tilt; still non-scalable by design
