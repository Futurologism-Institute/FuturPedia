Jewish Philosophy

Jewish philosophy refers to philosophical inquiry shaped by Jewish texts, traditions, and historical experience. It includes both the use of philosophy to understand Judaism and the contribution of Jewish thinkers to broader philosophical questions.

Key periods and figures include:

  • Hellenistic period: Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE – 50 CE), who synthesized Jewish scripture with Greek (especially Platonic and Stoic) philosophy.
  • Medieval period: Saadia Gaon (882–942), the first systematic Jewish philosopher; Solomon Ibn Gabirol; Judah Halevi; and Moses Maimonides (1138–1204), whose Guide of the Perplexed remains a central work. Later medieval thinkers include Gersonides and Hasdai Crescas.
  • Modern period: Baruch Spinoza, Moses Mendelssohn, Hermann Cohen, Franz Rosenzweig, Martin Buber, and Emmanuel Levinas, among others.

Recurring themes include the relationship between reason and revelation, the nature of God, creation, prophecy, free will, ethics, and the meaning of Jewish peoplehood and law (Halakhah).

Jewish philosophy has interacted extensively with Islamic, Christian, and secular Western philosophical traditions while remaining rooted in Jewish sources and concerns.
