Mathematical Future Framework for Asian Demographic & Social Statistics A Government Statistical Framework for Measuring Asian Populations, Outcomes, Inequality, Mobility, and Social Conditions in the United States ⸻ I. Executive Decision The United States should establish a Mathematical Future Asian Population Statistics Framework (MF-APSF) for measuring demographic, economic, educational, health, criminal-justice, immigration, housing, political, and social outcomes among Asian populations. The fundamental statistical principle is: \boxed{\text{Asian Aggregate} \neq \text{Asian Population}} An aggregate statistic can conceal enormous variation between Chinese, Asian Indian, Filipino, Vietnamese, Korean, Japanese, Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Cambodian, Hmong, Thai, Taiwanese, Nepalese, and other populations. The framework therefore requires government statistics to preserve information about: \text{Race} + \text{Ethnicity} + \text{National Origin} + \text{Ancestry} + \text{Immigration Generation} + \text{Language} + \text{Geography} + \text{Socioeconomic Status} + \text{Time} rather than treating “Asian” as a single explanatory variable. The Census Bureau currently defines race as a social/statistical classification rather than a biological or genetic classification, and race data are based on self-identification. ⸻ II. The Mathematical Future Principle The conventional statistical pipeline is: \text{Person} \rightarrow \text{Race Category} \rightarrow \text{Statistic} The Mathematical Future framework replaces it with: \boxed{ \text{Person} \rightarrow \text{Identity} \rightarrow \text{Classification} \rightarrow \text{Observation} \rightarrow \text{Context} \rightarrow \text{Estimate} \rightarrow \text{Uncertainty} } The observed statistic is therefore modeled as: Y = f( A, R, N, G, I, L, S, T, X )+\epsilon where: * A = ancestry/origin * R = racial identification * N = national origin * G = geography * I = immigration/generation status * L = language * S = socioeconomic characteristics * T = time * X = other contextual variables * \epsilon = measurement error. ⸻ III. The First Problem: What Does “Asian” Mean? This is the foundational problem. Under the 2024 OMB standards, Asian refers to people with origins in Central or East Asia, Southeast Asia, or South Asia. The updated federal standards also require additional detail beyond the minimum categories in many circumstances. Therefore: Asian = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} Asian_i but: P(Y|Asian) does not imply: P(Y|Asian_i)=P(Y|Asian_j) for all i,j. That is the Pan-Asian Aggregation Problem. ⸻ IV. The Disaggregation Principle Every major federal statistic concerning Asians should, whenever statistically feasible, be presented at multiple levels. Level 1 — Pan-Asian A Level 2 — Regional A_{East}, A_{South}, A_{Southeast}, A_{Central} Level 3 — National-origin A_{Chinese}, A_{Indian}, A_{Filipino}, A_{Vietnamese}, A_{Korean}, A_{Japanese}, ... Level 4 — Generational 1^{st},2^{nd},3^{rd}+ \text{ generation} Level 5 — Geographic National \rightarrow State \rightarrow County \rightarrow Metro Level 6 — Socioeconomic Income,\ Education,\ Occupation,\ Wealth,\ Housing This allows the government to distinguish: \boxed{\text{Within-Group Variation}} from \boxed{\text{Between-Group Variation}} ⸻ V. The Asian Aggregation Paradox Suppose two Asian populations have outcomes: A_1=90 and A_2=50. The aggregate might be: A=70. But the number 70 describes neither population. More importantly, if the population weights change: w_1(t),w_2(t) then: A(t)=w_1(t)A_1(t)+w_2(t)A_2(t). The aggregate can change even if neither group’s underlying outcome changes. Therefore: \boxed{ \Delta Aggregate \neq \Delta Individual\ Groups } This is a form of Simpson’s paradox / compositional change. ⸻ VI. Demographic Statistics The framework should separately measure: * population size * population growth * fertility * mortality * age structure * sex ratio * migration * immigration * emigration * citizenship * naturalization * household structure * geographic concentration * intermarriage * multiracial identification * language * ancestry * national origin. For example: P_{Asian}(t) = \sum_i P_{Asian_i}(t) but the growth rate is: g_i(t)= \frac{P_i(t)-P_i(t-1)} {P_i(t-1)}. The government should report both. ⸻ VII. Immigration Selection Bias Asian populations in the United States are not necessarily a random sample of the populations from their countries of origin. Let: P(A) represent the population in the country of origin. Immigration produces: P(A|Selected\ Migrant) which may be substantially different. Thus: \boxed{ Immigrant\ Population \neq Origin\ Country\ Population } This matters enormously when interpreting: * education * income * entrepreneurship * occupation * health * political behavior * language * fertility. ⸻ VIII. The Education Problem Government statistics should distinguish: Education_{Asian} from: Education_{Asian_i}. Important variables include: * parental education * household income * immigrant generation * English proficiency * school district * selective-school access * public/private schooling * college enrollment * college completion * graduate education * field of study. A simple model: Education = \alpha+ \beta_1 Origin+ \beta_2 Income+ \beta_3 Generation+ \beta_4 Geography+ \beta_5 School+ \epsilon. This prevents ethnicity from becoming a proxy for socioeconomic variables. ⸻ IX. Income and Wealth Income statistics should distinguish: MeanIncome from: MedianIncome and: MeanWealth from: MedianWealth. The government should also report: \[ Income_{per\capita} \] and: Income_{household} because household size varies significantly across populations. A household-income comparison can therefore produce misleading conclusions if household composition is ignored. ⸻ X. The “Model Minority” Statistical Problem The framework should explicitly prohibit using an aggregate Asian statistic to imply universal socioeconomic success. For example: MedianIncome_{Asian}>MedianIncome_{US} does not imply: Poverty_{Asian_i}
